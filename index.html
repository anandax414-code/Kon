One language. Seven scripts.

Konkani has been called the only Indian language with credible literature in half a dozen writing systems. Trace how Brahmi, Modi, Devanagari, Roman, Kannada, Malayalam, and Perso-Arabic rose, competed, and receded across Goa, coastal Karnataka, Kerala, and Maharashtra — from 2nd-century inscriptions to today's official-language disputes.

Reading the evidence

Where the sources disagree

Konkani's script history is contested territory — literally the subject of ongoing political disputes. These are the places where sources conflict, so you can weigh the claims yourself.

The 981 AD inscription

Some scholars attribute the Shravanabelagola inscription to Konkani; many linguists read its language as indistinguishable from Old Marathi of the Yadava era (1200–1300 CE).

Konkani language, Wikipedia →

The "1548 language ban"

Often cited as when Portuguese authorities banned Konkani. The 1548 letter actually documents book destruction — the formal prohibition decree dates to 27 June 1684.

Konkani language agitation, Wikipedia →

Six martyrs, or seven?

Accounts of the 1986–87 script agitation name six Catholic victims in one telling, while Roman-script advocacy groups consistently speak of "seven martyrs."

Herald Goa →

Inflated speaker estimates

Community advocacy cites 3.7 million Konkani speakers in Karnataka and 1 million in Kerala — versus census figures of 788,294 and 69,449 respectively.

Outlook →

Is Perso-Arabic still used?

One account says Perso-Arabic Konkani publishing ended roughly 50 years ago. But the fortnightly Naqsh-e-Nawayath has published continuously since 1976.

TwoCircles →

"A language in search of a script"

This popular phrase couldn't be traced to any source. Closer sourced framings: K. V. Narayana called Konkani "probably the only scriptless Indian language which uses nearly half-a-dozen scripts."
